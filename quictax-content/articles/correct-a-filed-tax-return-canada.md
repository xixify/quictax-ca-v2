# How to Correct a Filed Tax Return in Canada

A notice arrives, a receipt turns up, or you spot a number that does not look right. The good news: you can usually **correct a filed tax return** without starting from scratch. For Canadian taxpayers, the right move depends on what needs fixing, how you filed, and whether the CRA has already assessed your return.

A correction can affect your refund, balance owing, credits, deductions, and future tax records. Handle it promptly, keep clear documentation, and avoid filing a second return just to replace the first one. Fast filing is great. Accurate filing is better.

## When you should correct a filed tax return

Not every minor concern requires an adjustment. If the CRA already has the information and your return was calculated correctly, there may be nothing to change. But you should consider an adjustment when you missed income, entered the wrong amount, forgot a deduction or credit, or received a tax slip after filing.

Common reasons include a late T4, T4A, T5, or investment slip; unreported freelance or gig income; forgotten RRSP contributions; child care expenses; eligible medical expenses; tuition amounts; or work-from-home and business expenses. Self-employed taxpayers may also need to correct income, vehicle expenses, home office costs, capital cost allowance, or GST/HST figures.

A corrected return is not only about getting a larger refund. If you left out income, making the change quickly can reduce the risk of interest and penalties. The CRA may also reassess information it receives from employers, banks, platforms, or other issuers. Correcting the issue yourself is usually the cleaner path.

## First, wait for your Notice of Assessment

If you filed recently, do not rush to submit changes while your original return is still being processed. Wait until you receive your Notice of Assessment, or see that the assessment is complete in your CRA account. That document shows what the CRA accepted, changed, or questioned.

Read it carefully. A different refund amount does not always mean the CRA made a mistake. It may have adjusted an amount based on information already on file, applied an outstanding balance, or limited a claim because it needs more support.

If you simply entered a wrong number, an adjustment is usually appropriate. If you disagree with a CRA decision after reviewing the assessment and supporting documents, the situation may call for an objection instead. An adjustment asks the CRA to change information on the return. An objection formally disputes an assessment. They are not the same process.

## Gather the right records before you make changes

The fastest corrections start with complete paperwork. Find the original tax return, your Notice of Assessment, and the document that explains the error. If you are changing a deduction, keep the receipt, invoice, logbook, statement, or calculation that supports the revised amount.

For self-employed people, organize the change by category. For example, if you missed a business expense, identify the supplier, date, business purpose, tax amount, and proof of payment. If you are correcting income, confirm whether the amount is gross revenue, net revenue after platform fees, or an amount already reported elsewhere.

Do not estimate simply because a receipt is inconvenient to find. A reasonable reconstruction may be possible in some situations, but records remain the best protection if the CRA asks questions later. Keep your tax documents for at least six years after the end of the tax year they relate to.

## Choose the right way to change your return

Most personal tax return corrections can be requested online through [CRA My Account](https://quictax.ca/cra), or through ReFILE if your tax software supports it and your return qualifies. Online changes are often the most practical option because you can see the tax year, revise specific lines, and submit the request electronically.

If online adjustment is not available for your situation, you can use Form T1-ADJ, T1 Adjustment Request, or send a signed letter with the relevant details and supporting documents. The CRA may ask for more information before processing the change.

Your approach can differ for business filings. A sole proprietor reports business income on a personal return, so the personal-return adjustment process generally applies. A corporation needs to amend its T2 corporate return through the appropriate corporate adjustment process. GST/HST corrections can also follow different rules depending on whether the error is being fixed on a future return or through a separate adjustment request.

That distinction matters. A missed $400 software subscription on a freelance return is not handled the same way as an incorrect corporate income amount or an HST reporting error. When the filing is more complex, getting the correction prepared accurately can save more time than trying to untangle it alone.

## How to submit a clean CRA adjustment

Before submitting, compare the original figure with the new figure and make sure the change is reflected everywhere it should be. A revised business expense can change your net self-employment income, CPP contribution, taxable income, and refund or balance owing. One number often has a ripple effect.

Use a clear explanation. State the tax year, the line or schedule being changed, the original amount, the revised amount, and the reason for the correction. Attach or retain supporting documents as required. Clear records reduce back-and-forth and help the CRA understand exactly what you are requesting.

Avoid these common mistakes:

- Filing a completely new return instead of requesting an adjustment.

- Changing a number without checking related schedules and tax credits.

- Claiming an expense that was reimbursed or is personal rather than business-related.

- Sending incomplete documents or unclear explanations.

- Assuming a revised refund will arrive immediately.

After you submit the request, save the confirmation number, a copy of the revised calculation, and every document used to support it. Processing times vary. Check your CRA account for updates rather than submitting repeated requests for the same change.

## What happens after the CRA reviews it

The CRA may accept the adjustment, ask for supporting documents, or make a different change than you expected. Once the review is complete, you should receive a Notice of Reassessment. This replaces the relevant details from the earlier assessment and shows the revised refund, balance owing, credits, and carryforward amounts.

If the correction creates tax owing, pay as soon as you can. Interest may apply from the original payment deadline, even if you discover the error later. If you cannot pay the full amount right away, do not ignore it. Review your options and take action before the balance grows.

If your correction increases a refund, remember that the CRA can apply it to certain outstanding government debts. It can also review the claim before issuing payment. A larger refund is possible, but it is never guaranteed just because an adjustment was submitted.

## Extra care for freelancers and small-business owners

Business tax corrections deserve a closer look because records connect across more than one obligation. Revenue reported on your personal return may need to align with invoices, payment-platform statements, bookkeeping records, and GST/HST filings. A change to one area can expose an inconsistency in another.

For example, a consultant who forgot to report a project payment should correct the income, review related expenses, and confirm whether GST/HST was charged or should have been charged. A driver who finds missed mileage records should separate business travel from personal use and make sure the vehicle claim is calculated properly.

This is where a quick review can prevent a partial fix. The goal is not to claim every possible expense at any cost. It is to report income accurately, claim legitimate deductions with support, and keep your filing in compliance.

## Get the correction right without the runaround

A filed return is not permanently locked. Most errors can be corrected, but the details matter. Start with your Notice of Assessment, collect the evidence, use the appropriate adjustment method, and keep a complete record of what changed.
