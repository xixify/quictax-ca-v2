# How to Calculate Self Employment CPP in Canada

Your CPP bill can be one of the biggest surprises of self-employment. Employees see CPP taken off every paycheck. Freelancers, consultants, and sole proprietors usually see the full amount when they file. When you calculate self employment CPP for a Canadian tax return, you pay both the employee and employer portions - but you may also receive useful tax relief for part of that contribution.

For the 2025 tax year, the calculation depends on your net self-employment income, not your total sales. That distinction matters. Revenue is what clients paid you. Net income is what remains after eligible business expenses, and it is the number used for CPP.

## How to calculate self employment CPP for 2025

If your net self-employment income is more than $3,500, you generally contribute to the Canada Pension Plan. For 2025, the first CPP earnings ceiling is $71,300. The basic CPP rate for a self-employed person is 11.9% because you cover both sides of the contribution.

Start with this formula:

**Basic self-employed CPP = (net self-employment income - $3,500) × 11.9%**

That formula applies only until your income reaches the 2025 first earnings ceiling. The maximum basic CPP contribution for a self-employed person in 2025 is $8,068.20.

The $3,500 amount is called the basic exemption. It is not subject to CPP. If your net income is $3,500 or less, there is generally no self-employed CPP to pay. If your income is above that amount, CPP starts on the dollars over $3,500.

### Example: Net income of $45,000

Suppose you earned $45,000 in net income after claiming your eligible business expenses.

Your CPP calculation is: ($45,000 - $3,500) × 11.9% = **$4,938.50**.

That is the CPP amount added to your tax return calculation. It is not an extra fee charged by your tax preparer or a separate invoice you receive from the CRA. It is part of the balance calculated when you file.

### Example: Net income above the CPP ceiling

Now suppose your net business income was $90,000. You do not apply 11.9% to the entire $90,000. Basic CPP stops at the first earnings ceiling of $71,300.

Your maximum basic CPP would be: ($71,300 - $3,500) × 11.9% = **$8,068.20**.

But higher earners may have another CPP amount to consider: CPP2.

## CPP2: the additional contribution for higher income

CPP2 is the second additional CPP contribution. It applies to earnings above the first earnings ceiling, up to a second ceiling. For 2025, that range is from $71,300 to $81,200.

A self-employed person pays 8% on income in that range. The formula is:

**CPP2 = (income up to $81,200 - $71,300) × 8%**

The maximum CPP2 contribution for 2025 is $792. If your net self-employment income is $81,200 or more, your total maximum CPP is $8,860.20: $8,068.20 in basic CPP plus $792 in CPP2.

Using the $90,000 example, the CPP2 calculation is: ($81,200 - $71,300) × 8% = **$792**. Your total 2025 CPP is therefore **$8,860.20**.

CPP2 can feel frustrating when you are budgeting for taxes, but it is designed to increase future CPP retirement benefits. Whether that trade-off feels worthwhile depends on your cash flow today and your long-term retirement plans. Either way, it is a required calculation when your income reaches the threshold.

## Use net income, not gross sales

A common filing mistake is calculating CPP from every dollar deposited into your bank account. That can overstate your contribution and your taxes.

If you invoiced $80,000 but had $18,000 in legitimate business expenses, your net self-employment income is $62,000. Your basic CPP would be calculated on $62,000, not $80,000:

($62,000 - $3,500) × 11.9% = **$6,961.50**.

Your deductible expenses may include supplies, software, business-use vehicle costs, advertising, professional fees, home office expenses, and phone costs. The right deduction depends on how the expense relates to earning business income. Personal expenses do not become deductible simply because you are self-employed.

Accurate expense tracking can lower your taxable income and the CPP you owe. The goal is not to claim everything. The goal is to claim the business expenses you can support with records.

## What if you also have a job?

Many Canadians have both employment income and a side business. In that case, CPP deducted from your job affects how much CPP remains payable on your self-employment income.

Your employer will have already withheld CPP from your pay, and your employment income uses some or all of the annual CPP room. Your tax return calculation combines the information from your T4 with your self-employment income to determine the final amount. If you already reached the annual maximum through employment, you may not owe additional basic CPP on your business income. CPP2 can still apply depending on your total pensionable earnings.

This is one reason a quick online calculator may not give you the right answer. It may assume self-employment is your only income, overlook T4 deductions, or use outdated thresholds.

## CPP tax relief: you do not lose the full amount to tax

Self-employed CPP contributions receive tax treatment that helps reduce the impact. Broadly, part of your CPP contribution supports a non-refundable tax credit, while another portion can be deducted from income. The enhanced CPP amount is also treated differently than the base portion.

You do not need to split these amounts by hand when your return is prepared correctly. Still, it helps to understand why the CPP amount you calculate is not necessarily the same as its final after-tax cost.

The exact tax savings depend on your income, province or territory, and other deductions and credits. A larger CPP contribution does not mean you should avoid legitimate business income or expenses. It simply means planning matters.

## Keep these records before filing

A clean set of records makes the CPP calculation faster and more accurate. Keep your income records, invoices, expense receipts, mileage log if you use a vehicle for business, and home office details if you are claiming workspace costs. If you collect GST/HST, keep those records separate from your income and expenses as well.

Your T4 slips matter too if you worked for an employer during the year. They show CPP already deducted, which can change the final self-employment CPP calculation.

Do not wait until the filing deadline to assemble everything. A quick review before filing gives you time to spot missing expenses, correct income totals, and set aside funds for any balance owing.

## Plan for CPP before tax season

A practical rule is to set aside a percentage of every client payment in a separate savings account. The right percentage depends on your income, expenses, province or territory, GST/HST position, and whether you also have employment income. CPP is only one part of your tax bill, so saving only 11.9% is often not enough.

If your business income is growing, quarterly tax planning can prevent a stressful April surprise. Once the [CRA requires installments](https://quictax.ca/cra), paying them on time also helps you avoid interest.
