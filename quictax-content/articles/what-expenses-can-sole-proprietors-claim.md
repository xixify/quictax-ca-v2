# What Expenses Can Sole Proprietors Claim?

A $40 software subscription, a client lunch, a 300-mile drive, and a desk in your spare bedroom can all affect your tax bill. But only if they are legitimate business costs, properly calculated, and supported by records. Knowing **what expenses can sole proprietors claim** helps you keep more of what you earn without creating problems if the IRS asks questions later.

For U.S. sole proprietors, business income and expenses are generally reported on Schedule C with your individual tax return. The basic rule is simple: an expense must be ordinary and necessary for your business. Ordinary means it is common in your line of work. Necessary means it is helpful and appropriate. It does not have to be indispensable, but it does need a real business purpose.

## What expenses can sole proprietors claim on Schedule C?

The deductible expenses available to a sole proprietor depend on how the business operates. A freelance designer, rideshare driver, online retailer, and local contractor will not have the same costs. The goal is not to find every possible deduction. It is to report the expenses you actually paid to earn business income, accurately and confidently.

### Advertising, marketing, and sales costs

You can generally deduct reasonable costs used to promote or sell your business. That can include website hosting, domain renewals, online ads, printed materials, photography for product listings, logo design, email marketing platforms, and fees paid to a marketing consultant.

If you sponsor a local event or pay for social media advertising, keep the invoice and a note showing the business purpose. Personal social media subscriptions or purchases with no clear connection to your work should stay out of the business books.

### Office supplies, equipment, and software

Day-to-day supplies are commonly deductible. Think printer ink, shipping labels, packaging, stationery, notebooks, and small tools. Software used for bookkeeping, design, scheduling, video meetings, customer management, or project delivery can also qualify when it supports the business.

Equipment needs a little more attention. A laptop, camera, specialized machinery, office furniture, or high-value tool may be deducted immediately under available tax rules or depreciated over time. The best treatment depends on the item, its cost, how long you expect to use it, and current tax elections. Do not assume every large purchase belongs in the same expense category.

### Professional services and contractors

Fees paid for business support are generally deductible. This may include bookkeeping, tax preparation, legal advice, payroll support, web development, virtual assistant services, and payments to independent contractors.

If you pay a contractor $600 or more during the year, you may have information reporting responsibilities, such as issuing a Form 1099-NEC. Collecting a completed Form W-9 before you pay a contractor can make year-end reporting much easier.

### Rent, utilities, phone, and internet

Commercial office rent, coworking memberships, storage space, and workshop rent can be deductible when used for the business. Utilities for a dedicated business location may also qualify.

For a phone or home internet plan used for both personal and business purposes, deduct only the business portion. If your phone is used 70% for client calls, scheduling, and business messages, a 70% deduction may be reasonable. The key is using a method you can explain and applying it consistently.

### Business insurance, licenses, and banking fees

Many sole proprietors overlook the routine costs that keep a business operating. Business liability insurance, professional coverage, required licenses, permits, industry memberships, and business bank account fees can be deductible.

Merchant processing fees, payment platform charges, and interest on a business loan may also qualify. Keep statements that show the fees separately. If a loan or credit card is partly personal, only the business-related interest belongs on Schedule C.

## Vehicle and travel expenses need clean records

Vehicle deductions are valuable, but they are also an area where weak documentation causes trouble. Driving from your home to a client meeting, job site, supply store, or temporary work location may be business mileage. Your regular commute to a permanent workplace is generally personal and not deductible.

You usually choose between two methods for a vehicle used in your business: the standard mileage rate or actual expenses. The standard mileage rate is simpler because you track eligible business miles and apply the IRS rate for the year. The actual expense method uses the business share of costs such as fuel, repairs, insurance, registration, lease payments, and depreciation.

Neither method is automatically better. High-mileage drivers often prefer the standard rate, while vehicles with significant operating costs may benefit from actual expenses. Your mileage log should capture the date, destination, business purpose, and miles driven. Reconstructing a full year from memory is a risky plan.

Business travel can also be deductible when it is ordinary and necessary. Airfare, lodging, local transportation, baggage fees, and a portion of meals may qualify for an overnight trip that is primarily for business. A vacation does not become deductible because you answer a few emails while away. Separate personal days and personal costs carefully.

## The home office deduction can work if you qualify

Working from home does not automatically create a home office deduction. The space must generally be used regularly and exclusively for business, and it must be your principal place of business or meet another IRS requirement.

“Exclusively” matters. A kitchen table used for client work in the morning and family dinners at night usually does not qualify. A dedicated room or clearly defined workspace used only for business is a much stronger position.

Eligible home office costs can include a portion of rent, mortgage interest, property taxes, utilities, homeowners or renters insurance, repairs, and depreciation. Sole proprietors may use the simplified method, based on eligible square footage, or the actual expense method, based on the business percentage of home costs.

The simplified method is faster and reduces paperwork. The actual method may produce a larger deduction, especially where housing expenses are high. It depends on your numbers, so compare both before filing.

## Inventory, cost of goods, and education

If you sell physical products, the cost of inventory is not handled exactly like a general office expense. Items purchased for resale, materials used to make products, freight-in costs, and certain direct production costs may be part of cost of goods sold. Good inventory records matter because they affect both taxable profit and year-end reporting.

Training can be deductible when it maintains or improves skills in your existing business. A photographer taking an advanced editing course or a consultant attending an industry conference may have a valid deduction. Education that qualifies you for an entirely new profession is usually not deductible as a business expense.

## Expenses that are commonly personal or limited

A deduction is not a reward for spending money. Personal, living, and family costs are generally not deductible, even if being self-employed makes them feel connected to your work. Clothing is usually personal unless it is a required uniform or protective gear not suitable for everyday wear. Personal grooming, ordinary meals, fines, political contributions, and commuting are also commonly nondeductible.

Meals deserve extra care. Business meals may be partly deductible when there is a clear business purpose and proper documentation, but entertainment costs are generally not deductible. Save the receipt and write down who attended and what business was discussed.

## Build records that make deductions easy to defend

The strongest deduction is one you can prove. Keep receipts, invoices, bank statements, mileage logs, contracts, and payment confirmations throughout the year. A separate business bank account and dedicated business credit card make it much easier to sort transactions without mixing in personal purchases.

For each unusual or larger expense, add a short note about why it was needed for the business. That one habit can save hours at tax time. Digital copies are generally fine when they are readable, complete, and securely stored.

Tax deductions should reduce stress, not create it. Claim the costs that truly support your work, keep clean documentation, and ask a qualified tax professional before taking an aggressive position you cannot support. Accurate records give you a clearer view of your profit and a far easier filing season.
